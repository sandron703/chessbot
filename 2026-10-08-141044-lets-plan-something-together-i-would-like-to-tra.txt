  What's there

  Seven stages, each a standalone module, wired together by a Makefile:

  1 selfplay   stockfish self-play ........ data/selfplay/shard-*.jsonl.zst
  2 prepare    tokenize ...................
  ../nanoGPT/data/chess_uci/{train,val}.bin + meta.pkl
  3 train      nanoGPT, UNMODIFIED ........ ../nanoGPT/out-chess-small/ckpt.pt
  4 evaluate   loss / legality / ACPL / Elo / bench
  5 uci        the model as a real chess engine on stdio
  6 export     int8 quantize .............. out/chess-small.gptc
  7 deploy     rsync to the UNO Q + bench there

  Two decisions drive the design, both documented in prototype./README.md:

  Square-pair tokens — a move is from,to (+promo). Vocab 80, 2.05 tokens/ply. A
  full move vocabulary (~1968) would eat 16% of the small model's parameter
  budget on the embedding table alone.

  Legality-constrained decoding (chessgpt/masking.py) — this is what square
  pairs buy. Each slot is intersected with what chess allows, so an illegal move
  is unrepresentable. And rather than sampling an origin and getting stuck with
  it, it scores every legal move as log P(from) + log P(to|from) + log 
  P(promo|·) and normalizes over the legal set — the model's true conditional
  distribution restricted to legal chess, ~8 forward passes per move sharing one
  KV cache.

  Verified, not assumed

    with random weights, .gptc roundtrip.
  - make smoke runs all seven stages from clean — 400 games → tokenize → train →

❯ can you explain to me what step, train and val are?                           

  Ran 2 shell commands (ctrl+o to expand)

● Sure. All three come from nanoGPT's training loop, and they mean quite
  specific things.

  step — one optimizer update

  Not one game, not one pass through the data. Each step the trainer grabs
  batch_size=64 random windows of block_size=384 tokens and does one weight
  update:

  64 windows × 384 tokens = 24,576 tokens per step

  Your training set is 52,774,797 tokens, so:

  - one epoch (one pass over all the data) = 52,774,797 ÷ 24,576 = 2,147 steps
  - 12,000 steps = 5.6 passes over the same games

  So "step 7500" means 7,500 weight updates, ~184M tokens consumed, a bit under
  3.5 times through your 333k games.

  loss — how surprised the model was

  At every position the model predicts the next token. Loss is the average of
  −ln(probability it assigned to the token that actually came next), in nats per
  token. Lower is better. The useful trick is exp(loss), which gives perplexity
  — roughly, how many equally-likely options it's effectively choosing between:

  ┌───────────────────────────────────────┬──────────┬───────────┐
  │                                       │ val loss │ exp(loss) │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼───────────┤
  │ step 0 (random init)                  │ 4.4676   │ 87.2      │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼───────────┤
  │ uniform guess over the 80-token vocab │ 4.3820   │ 80.0      │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼───────────┤
  │ step 1250                             │ 1.7576   │ 5.8       │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼───────────┤
  │ step 7500                             │ 1.3600   │ 3.9       │
  └───────────────────────────────────────┴──────────┴───────────┘

  At step 0 it's guessing blind — 87 is essentially "no idea", the whole
  vocabulary. At step 7500 it's narrowed 64 possible squares down to about 4 
  real candidates per slot. That's the Sicilian you saw: when it recaptured cxd4
  at 99% probability, that's a loss near 0.01 on that one token.

  train vs val — the memorization check

  Two different sets of games, same measurement:

  - train loss — measured on the 332,618 games it's actively fitting
  - val loss — measured on the 3,000 games prepare held out and wrote to
    val_games.jsonl.zst. The model has never seen them.

  The number that matters is the gap. At step 7500 it's 1.3453 vs 1.3600 — a gap
  of 0.015. That means it's learning chess patterns that generalize, not
  memorizing specific games. If train kept falling while val flattened or turned
  upward, that's overfitting, and the fix here would be easy: chess_uci_all has
  35% more tokens sitting ready.

  Val is also what decides whether a checkpoint gets written. Your config sets
  always_save_checkpoint = False, so out-chess-small/ckpt.pt is only overwritten
  when val loss hits a new best — you can't end up with a worse model than the
  best one seen.

  One gotcha in the log

  There are two kinds of line and they are not comparable:

  iter 7490: loss 1.4522, time 61.68ms      <- ONE minibatch. Noisy.
  step 7500: train loss 1.3453, val 1.3600  <- average of 100 batches per split
  iter 7500: loss 1.4456, time 4497.41ms    <- note the 4.5s: that's the eval

  The iter lines are a single random batch of 64 windows, so they bounce around
  ±0.1 and read systematically higher than the smoothed train loss. The step
  lines come from estimate_loss(), which averages eval_iters=100 batches from
  each split every eval_interval=250 steps — that's also why one iteration every
  250 takes 4.5 s instead of 61 ms.

  Worth keeping in mind

  Loss is a proxy, not the goal. It measures "did you predict the move a
  2400-rated human played", which is not the same as "did you play a good move".
  Loss 1.36 doesn't tell you it opens with the Sicilian; the 92% unmasked legal
  rate and (once training finishes) ACPL and match Elo are what actually say
  whether it plays chess. That's why evaluate.py has five metrics instead of
  one, and why it also breaks loss down by slot — from-square and to-square

  loses, and it almost never wins. I dug into how the games end:

  - Against Stockfish: 21/30 losses by checkmate, 9/30 draws all by threefold

    mostly repeating to save lost positions, not throwing away won ones.
  - Against random: it reaches overwhelming positions and then shuffles.

  Mean ACPL is 317 against a median of 46 — that gap is 15.5% of moves being 
  >300cp blunders. Mostly sound play, punctuated by disasters.

  This is structural, not a training bug. Next-token prediction with no search
  cannot calculate a mate, and endgames are the sparse tail of the data: every
  game shares an opening, while winning technique is diverse and rare.
